If your parent's doctor has recommended checking blood pressure at home, the choices can be confusing. This guide compares upper arm monitors on accuracy, ease of use and price.

High blood pressure increases the risk of heart disease and stroke, and it usually has no warning signs or symptoms, so many people do not know they have it (CDC). A reliable home blood pressure monitor is one of the most important health tools an aging parent can own, letting them track readings between doctor visits and catch dangerous spikes before they become emergencies.

We compared the five blood pressure monitors below, cross-referencing manufacturer specifications, the validated device list at validatebp.org, and published reviews. Here's what the research shows.

What Seniors Actually Need (Buying Guide)

1. Always Choose Upper Arm Over Wrist

Wrist monitors look convenient, but they require the wrist to be held at exactly heart level during a reading: a position that's difficult to maintain, especially for seniors with arthritis or limited mobility. Even a slight deviation causes significant inaccuracy. Every monitor on our list is an upper arm model, and we recommend the same for any purchase.

2. Clinical Validation Is Non-Negotiable

Many cheap blood pressure monitors on Amazon have not been independently validated for accuracy. A monitor that reads well off your actual blood pressure gives false reassurance, or false panic. The American Heart Association recommends choosing a validated monitor: check that the exact model you plan to buy is on the validated device list at validatebp.org. All five of our picks are upper arm models.

3. AFib Detection Matters More Than Most People Know

Atrial fibrillation (AFib) becomes more common with age. Many have no idea because it causes no obvious symptoms. The American Heart Association reports that people with AFib are five times more likely to have a stroke than people without it. Every monitor on our list includes an irregular heartbeat indicator: a simple icon that prompts further discussion with a doctor. For seniors, this feature should not be optional. Some smartwatches can also flag an irregular heart rhythm: see our best smartwatches for seniors.

4. Cuff Size: Get This Right First

An incorrectly sized cuff is the single most common cause of inaccurate home blood pressure readings. Before buying, measure your parent's upper arm circumference at the midpoint between elbow and shoulder. Standard adult cuffs typically cover 9 to 13 inches; large adult cuffs cover 13 to 17 inches. Many older adults need a large cuff. The Greater Goods 0602 is the only monitor on our list that ships with both sizes included.

5. Do They Need Bluetooth?

Bluetooth and WiFi connectivity add value in one specific scenario: a family member wants to remotely track readings over time via an app. If your parent lives alone and you check in on their health from a distance, the Omron Connect or Withings app is useful. If the goal is simply accurate daily readings that your parent records themselves, the Omron 3 Series at $35 does the job without any app complexity. Many families pair readings with a pill organizer so medication and blood pressure are checked together.

6. How to Take an Accurate Reading

  • Sit quietly for 5 minutes first: rushing straight from activity inflates the reading significantly
  • No caffeine or exercise in the 30 minutes prior
  • Sit with back supported and feet flat on the floor
  • Rest the arm on a table at heart level: not held up in the air
  • Take two readings, 1 minute apart: average them for the most accurate result
  • Take readings at the same time each day: blood pressure fluctuates naturally through the day

7. When to Call a Doctor Immediately

Call 911 or seek emergency care if your parent's reading is 180/120 mmHg or higher, especially with symptoms like chest pain, shortness of breath, back pain, numbness, or visual changes. This is a hypertensive crisis. Do not wait to see if it improves on its own. If your parent lives alone, a medical alert system can place that emergency call for them.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the most accurate home blood pressure monitor for seniors?
Upper arm monitors are consistently more accurate than wrist monitors for seniors. The American Heart Association recommends automatic upper-arm monitors and advises against wrist and finger monitors, which give less reliable readings. Our top pick, the Omron Platinum BP5465, is an upper-arm monitor with AFib screening and a large backlit display.
Should seniors use a wrist or upper arm blood pressure monitor?
Upper arm monitors are strongly recommended for seniors. Wrist monitors require the wrist to be held exactly at heart level to be accurate: a position seniors with arthritis or limited mobility often struggle to maintain. Upper arm monitors are more forgiving and consistently more accurate across all five of our picks.
How often should a senior check their blood pressure at home?
Most doctors recommend checking twice in the morning before medication and meals, and twice in the evening, for 7 days before a medical appointment. For ongoing monitoring, once daily in the morning is typical. Always follow your parent's doctor's specific guidance, as frequency varies based on their condition and medications.
What blood pressure reading is dangerous for a senior?
A reading above 180/120 mmHg is a hypertensive crisis requiring immediate medical attention, call 911. A reading consistently above 130/80 mmHg indicates hypertension to discuss with a doctor. Readings below 90/60 mmHg indicate low blood pressure, which can cause dizziness and falls in seniors, also worth a doctor's call.
Can a blood pressure monitor detect AFib?
Yes, several monitors including the Omron Platinum and Omron Gold include an irregular heartbeat detection feature that flags potential AFib during a reading. This is not a medical diagnosis, but it alerts you to discuss the finding with a doctor. AFib is common in seniors over 65 and significantly increases stroke risk, so this feature is worth having.
What cuff size do I need for a senior blood pressure monitor?
Measure the upper arm at the midpoint between shoulder and elbow. Standard adult cuffs fit 9 to 13 inches; large adult cuffs fit 13 to 17 inches. Many older adults need a large cuff. An incorrect cuff size is the most common cause of inaccurate home readings, always verify the fit. The Greater Goods 0602 ships with both sizes included.
Does Medicare cover blood pressure monitors for seniors?
Generally, no. Original Medicare does not cover standard home blood pressure monitors for most beneficiaries. The main exception is an ambulatory blood pressure monitor (ABPM), which Medicare Part B may cover once a year, but only when a doctor orders it to check for suspected white coat hypertension: a different, doctor-administered device, not a home cuff monitor. Since January 2024, Medicare Part B also covers self-measured blood pressure monitoring training and education, but not the monitor itself. Some Medicare Advantage plans include an over-the-counter (OTC) allowance that can be put toward a monitor, worth checking your parent's specific plan benefits.
Omron Platinum vs Omron Gold: which should I buy?
The Omron Platinum BP5465 (~$79) and Omron Gold BP5350 (~$59) are almost identical: both detect AFib, both store 200 readings for 2 users, both sync to the Omron Connect app. One difference: the Platinum has a backlit display and the Gold does not, which makes readings harder in dim rooms. For most seniors whose blood pressure is well-controlled, the Omron Gold is the smarter buy: the $20 saved is real money for no meaningful difference in daily use. Choose the Platinum if your parent takes readings in poor lighting or has readings that frequently exceed 160 mmHg.
