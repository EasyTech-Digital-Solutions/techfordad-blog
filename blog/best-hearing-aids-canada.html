Best Hearing Aids in Canada 2026: Reviewed & Ranked

We compared the top hearing aids available to Canadians: prescription brands like Phonak and ReSound, Costco Canada's value options, and the new OTC options, with Canadian prices in CAD and a province-by-province subsidy guide.

Turning the TV up very loud is often the first sign of hearing loss. In Canada, choosing hearing aids also means understanding provincial funding, audiologists, Costco and over the counter options. This guide covers each one.

Hearing loss is one of the most common, and most commonly ignored, health concerns facing Canadian seniors. In a Statistics Canada health survey, 78% of adults aged 60 to 79 had measurable hearing loss when even slight loss is counted. The barrier is often cost: a quality pair of prescription hearing aids in Canada typically runs between $2,500 and $6,000 CAD.

The good news: Canada has meaningful provincial subsidy programs, Costco Canada offers excellent value through its Hearing Centres, and as of 2026, British Columbia became the first province to allow over-the-counter (OTC) hearing aids: a shift that is expected to spread to other provinces within the next year or two. This guide covers the best hearing aids available in Canada right now, with Canadian prices and the provincial funding information you need to make an informed decision.

If you're in the US or researching general options, our US hearing aids guide covers the American market separately. For seniors looking for broader health technology options, our Canadian medical alert systems guide is also worth a read.

Phonak is a Swiss brand (owned by Sonova) and one of the most widely dispensed hearing aids in Canada. Infinio is their current flagship rechargeable receiver-in-canal (RIC) platform, replacing the previous Lumity generation, and it consistently tops audiologist and user review lists for 2026. The reason: AutoSense OS 6.0, running on the new ERA chip, uses machine learning to detect your acoustic environment and adjust settings automatically, so your hearing aids perform differently in a quiet home than in a noisy restaurant without you having to press anything. (Lumity is still sold by some Canadian clinics at a lower price point for buyers who don't need the newest technology.)

For Canadian seniors who struggle most with understanding speech at family dinners, at church, or in busy environments like Costco (the store, not the hearing centre), Audéo Infinio is routinely recommended by audiologists as the most effective option on the market. The rechargeable case provides a full 24-hour charge, which matters for seniors who don't want to handle tiny disposable batteries.

Phonak is available at independent audiologist clinics across Canada and at HearingLife Canada locations in most provinces. If Ontario's ADP applies to you, your audiologist can help you apply: the $500/ear subsidy ($1,000/pair) meaningfully reduces the out-of-pocket cost.

ReSound (a Danish brand owned by GN Audio) is widely available in Canada and consistently recommended for seniors who want prescription-grade quality without paying for top-tier technology they may not need. The Vivia model is particularly popular for tinnitus sufferers: the ringing or buzzing sound that commonly accompanies hearing loss. The built-in Sound Therapy feature plays gentle background tones or nature sounds to mask tinnitus, which many wearers find provides meaningful relief.

For seniors whose primary hearing challenge is following conversations one-on-one rather than in large crowds, the ReSound Vivia offers outstanding value compared to premium options. At $2,500 to $4,400 CAD per pair, it sits in the middle ground where you get Bluetooth streaming, rechargeability, and professional fitting at a more accessible price. After Ontario ADP ($1,000/pair) or Alberta AADL funding, the out-of-pocket cost for many Canadians becomes much more manageable.

Oticon (Danish, now owned by Demant) is another major brand sold through Canadian audiologists, alongside Phonak. The Intent model uses a Deep Neural Network that Oticon says was trained on more than 12 million real-world sound scenes to separate speech from background noise in a fundamentally different way than traditional hearing aids. The 4D Sensor detects head movement, so when your parent turns their head to listen to someone, the aids automatically prioritize sound from that direction.

For seniors who find family dinners or large gatherings particularly difficult (multiple voices, background music, dishes clattering) the Oticon Intent is often the recommendation. It's a premium product at a premium price, but for the right person, the improvement in real-world speech understanding can make a big difference.

Oticon is available through audiologists in all provinces. If your parent is in Quebec, Oticon Canada has a strong presence through CISSS-affiliated audiology clinics. Your CLSC can point you in the right direction.

Costco Canada Hearing Centres are arguably the single biggest change for hearing aid access in Canada. Prices at Costco start around $2,100 CAD per pair: compared to $3,500 to $6,000 at a private clinic for comparable technology. The difference is Costco's business model: they negotiate volume pricing with manufacturers and charge less markup. The hearing instrument specialists on staff are licensed professionals, not salespeople.

The brands Costco Canada currently carries (Philips HearLink, Jabra Enhance Pro, and Rexton) are all built on the same underlying platform as GN and Signia prescription aids. These are not budget devices. Philips hearing aids, for example, are made by Demant (same parent company as Oticon) and offer excellent sound quality at Costco prices.

The catch: Costco Hearing Centres are only in locations with a Costco Wholesale, which means they're strong in major cities (Toronto, Vancouver, Calgary, Edmonton, Montreal, Ottawa) but unavailable in smaller communities. If your parent lives in a rural area, a private audiologist is likely the only option.

One important note for Ontario families: ADP only pays when you use an ADP-registered vendor and follow the application steps before buying, so ask Costco, or any clinic you are considering, whether it is registered and what to bring.

Over-the-Counter Hearing Aids in Canada: 2026 Update

This is an area changing fast. Here's what you need to know:

British Columbia leads the way

In June 2026, BC became the first province in Canada to allow the sale of OTC hearing aidsto adults 18 and older with mild to moderate hearing loss, without requiring a prescription or visit to an audiologist. The devices are Health Canada-approved and can be purchased at select retailers and online.

Ontario is next

We have not found a matching rule change in other provinces. Check your province's rules before buying an OTC hearing aid, and see an audiologist first if your parent has more than mild to moderate hearing loss.

Apple AirPods Pro 2: Not Yet Available in Canada as OTC Hearing Aids

Health Canada licensed the hearing aid feature built into AirPods Pro 2 earbuds on December 11, 2024 (MacRumors), but Apple said in July 2025 that "due to regulatory restrictions" it could not release the feature in Canada (iPhone in Canada). We have not confirmed any change since, so check Apple Canada's site for the current status.

Our take on OTC for Canadian seniors

OTC hearing aids are best suited for adults with mild to moderate hearing loss who are tech-comfortable enough to use a smartphone app for self-fitting. If your parent has more significant hearing loss, or if they find technology frustrating, a prescription hearing aid fitted by an audiologist will almost always deliver a better outcome. The provincial subsidy programs (Ontario ADP, Alberta AADL, Manitoba) apply to prescription aids. Many OTC devices do not yet qualify.

Canadian Hearing Aid Buying Guide

Start with a proper hearing test

Before buying anything, your parent needs an audiogram: a professional hearing test that maps their specific hearing loss across different frequencies. In some provinces, audiology assessments may be covered by the provincial health plan when referred by a family doctor; check your province. The audiogram tells you the degree and type of hearing loss, which determines which aids are appropriate. Don't skip this step.

Understanding hearing aid types

  • RIC / RITE (Receiver-in-Canal / Receiver-in-the-Ear): The most popular style for seniors. A small unit sits behind the ear with a thin wire leading to a tiny speaker in the ear canal. Comfortable, discreet, and compatible with Bluetooth. Most of the products in this guide are RIC style.
  • BTE (Behind-the-Ear): Larger unit, more powerful, often recommended for severe to profound hearing loss. More visible but easier to handle for seniors with dexterity issues.
  • ITE / ITC / CIC (In-the-Ear styles): Custom-molded to fit entirely in the ear. More discreet but harder to handle, and some models don't offer Bluetooth.

Rechargeable vs. disposable batteries

We almost always recommend rechargeable hearing aids for Canadian seniors. Disposable batteries (size 312 or 10) are tiny and difficult to handle, especially with arthritis. Rechargeable aids sit in a charging case overnight: simpler, safer, and no battery runs out in the middle of the day. Most 2026 models offer rechargeable options.

Bluetooth matters: but check compatibility

Bluetooth streaming lets hearing aids receive audio directly from a smartphone or TV. Most modern hearing aids stream directly from iPhones; Android compatibility varies by brand. If your parent has an Android phone, confirm Bluetooth compatibility before purchasing. Ask your audiologist which models work with your parent's phone.

What to look for in a Canadian provider

  • Trial period: A minimum 30-day trial is standard in Canada; 60 to 90 days is better. Never buy without one.
  • What's included: Clarify whether follow-up adjustments, cleaning, and maintenance are included in the price or billed separately. Full-service audiology clinics typically bundle these in.
  • Warranty: Look for at least 2 to 3 years. Costco Canada offers a 3-year warranty; independent audiologists vary.
  • French language service: If your parent's first language is French, confirm the clinic offers service en français. This is particularly important in Quebec and parts of New Brunswick and Ontario.

Hearing aids and smartwatches

Some Canadian seniors combine hearing aids with a smartwatch for broader safety coverage. Our guide to smartwatches for seniorscovers fall detection and emergency SOS options: features that complement hearing aids without conflicting with them.

Province-by-Province Subsidy Guide

Canadian hearing aid subsidies vary considerably by province. The figures below reflect programs as of mid-2026, eligibility requirements and funding amounts change, so always verify current details directly with the program before relying on them.

Ontario: Assistive Devices Program (ADP)

The Ontario Assistive Devices Program (ADP) is the main provincial program. Ontario pays 75% of the cost of a hearing aid, up to $500 per side (up to $1,000 for a pair). You must be an Ontario resident with a valid health card and a hearing disability that needs a device for six months or longer; income is not considered. Get assessed by an ADP-registered audiologist, complete the application with them and buy from an ADP-registered vendor. Do these steps before purchasing, or ADP will not cover the cost. OHIP itself does not cover hearing aids.

Contact: ServiceOntario or ask your audiologist for an ADP referral form.

British Columbia: Medical Services Plan & Community Living BC

The BC government funds hearing aids for people eligible for general health supplements through income or disability assistance: up to $2,000 per aid, with a one-year supply of batteries included, arranged through the person's hearing provider. In June 2026, BC also became the first province or territory to expand access to Health Canada-approved over-the-counter hearing aids for adults 18 and older with mild to moderate hearing loss (CARP).

Contact: Your BC audiologist or MSP client relations for current eligibility criteria.

Alberta: Aids to Daily Living (AADL)

Alberta's AADL program is a cost-share model: clients pay 25% of the cost of program benefits, up to a maximum of $500 per family per benefit year, and low-income Albertans can apply for an exemption. Seniors and their adult dependents needing hearing aids can apply for the cost-share exemption using a dedicated form. Ask your audiologist which hearing aids qualify.

Contact: Alberta Health Services or your audiologist for AADL authorization forms.

Manitoba: Senior Hearing Aid Grant

Manitoba's Seniors Hearing Aid Program provides up to $2,000 toward hearing aids, the hearing assessment and fitting for people 65 and older whose household net income was below $80,000 in the previous year. It allows one claim per person every five years, and a certified hearing aid dealer licensed in Manitoba must prescribe the aids.

Contact: Manitoba Health or a Manitoba audiologist for current application details.

Quebec: RAMQ and Regional Programs

Quebec's Régie de l'assurance maladie du Québec (RAMQ) covers audiology assessments when referred by a physician. For hearing aid devices, contact your local CLSC (Centre local de services communautaires), regional health authorities (CISSS/CIUSSS) have their own programs for low-income seniors that aren't always widely advertised. Seniors enrolled in the Régime québécois d'assurance parentale or receiving low-income support may have additional options.

Contact:Your local CLSC: this is the single best starting point for Quebec seniors.

Other Provinces & Territories

  • Saskatchewan: Saskatchewan Aids to Independent Living (SAIL) covers some hearing aids for eligible residents. Contact your regional health authority.
  • Nova Scotia: The Hearing Aid Program provides support to low-income Nova Scotians. Contact the Department of Seniors and Long-term Care.
  • New Brunswick, PEI, Newfoundland: Each province has programs for children and low-income adults. Contact your provincial Department of Health or a local audiologist for current details.
  • Yukon, NWT, Nunavut: Extended Health Benefits through territorial health programs may cover hearing aids. Contact your territorial health authority.

Federal Programs

  • Veterans Affairs Canada: Veterans may receive full hearing aid coverage through the VAC hearing loss program. This can be substantial, contact VAC directly.
  • Non-Insured Health Benefits (NIHB): First Nations and Inuit individuals are covered for hearing aids under federal NIHB. Contact Health Canada's NIHB program.
  • Disability Tax Credit (DTC): Canadians with a severe and prolonged hearing impairment may be eligible: the Canada Revenue Agency says you must be unable to hear so as to understand spoken conversation in a quiet setting (or it takes you three times longer than someone your age) even with a hearing device, all or almost all of the time, for at least 12 months. A doctor, nurse practitioner or audiologist certifies it. Discuss it with your audiologist and a tax advisor.
  • Medical Expense Tax Credit (METC): Hearing aids, repairs and batteries are eligible medical expenses on your federal tax return, regardless of province (Canada Revenue Agency). Keep all receipts.

Our Recommendation for Canadian Families

The right hearing aid for your parent depends on four things: the degree of their hearing loss, your budget, your location (city vs. rural), and whether they're comfortable with technology.

For most Canadian seniors, we'd suggest this path: Start with a referral to an audiologist from the family doctor (often covered provincially). Get the audiogram. If you're in a city with Costco access and budget is the primary concern, Costco Canada Hearing is an excellent starting point, comparable technology at roughly half the price of private clinics. If the best possible speech understanding in difficult environments is the priority and budget allows, Phonak Audéo Infinio or Oticon Intent are the top-tier choices. For mild hearing loss, especially in BC where OTC aids are now available, or for seniors in rural areas, ask an audiologist whether remote follow-up is available.

Whatever you choose: take advantage of the trial period, apply for your provincial subsidy before purchasing (not after), and don't rush the fitting process, proper programming makes as much difference as the device itself.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are hearing aids covered by provincial health plans in Canada?
Coverage varies by province. Ontario's ADP pays 75% of the cost of a hearing aid, up to $500 per side. Manitoba's seniors program gives up to $2,000 toward hearing aids for people 65 and older with household net income under $80,000. Alberta's AADL is a cost-share program: clients pay 25% of the cost of benefits up to $500 per family per benefit year, with an exemption for low incomes. In BC, people eligible for general health supplements through income or disability assistance can get up to $2,000 per hearing aid. Federal programs cover Veterans and First Nations/Inuit. Always verify current eligibility directly with your provincial program, as these change regularly.
Can I buy over-the-counter hearing aids in Canada?
As of mid-2026, British Columbia became the first province to allow adults 18+ with mild to moderate hearing loss to purchase Health Canada-approved OTC hearing aids without a prescription. We have not found a matching change in other provinces, so check your province's rules before buying an OTC hearing aid.
What is the average cost of hearing aids in Canada?
Costco Canada Hearing Centres start around $2,100 CAD per pair. Mid-range prescription aids typically run $2,500 to $4,500 CAD per pair. Premium brands like Phonak and Oticon can be $4,000 to $6,000+ CAD per pair. Most prices at full-service clinics include fitting and follow-up adjustments.
Where is the best place to buy hearing aids in Canada?
Costco Canada Hearing Centres offer the best value (from ~$2,100/pair, free fitting included). Independent audiologists provide the most personalized care and widest brand selection. National chains like HearingLife Canada and HearCANADA are widely available. In BC, select retail locations now carry OTC hearing aids. Confirm what's included in the price (fitting, follow-ups, warranty) before purchasing anywhere.
Does OHIP cover hearing aids in Ontario?
OHIP does not cover hearing aids. However, Ontario's Assistive Devices Program (ADP) pays 75% of the cost of a hearing aid, up to $500 per side, and income is not considered. You must be assessed by an ADP-registered audiologist and complete the application steps before purchasing. Private employer insurance often provides additional coverage on top of ADP.
What is the difference between prescription and OTC hearing aids?
Prescription hearing aids are fitted and programmed by a licensed audiologist based on your specific audiogram. They work for all degrees of hearing loss. OTC hearing aids (available in BC as of 2026) are self-fitted using an app and are designed for mild to moderate hearing loss. For seniors with significant hearing loss or complex needs, prescription aids typically deliver better outcomes. Most provincial subsidy programs apply to prescription aids, not OTC devices.
