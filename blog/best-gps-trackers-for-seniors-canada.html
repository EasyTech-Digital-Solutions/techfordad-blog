If a parent with dementia has started wandering, families need real time location that works across Canada, not a Bluetooth tag that relies on nearby phones. This guide compares GPS trackers with Canadian coverage and CAD pricing.

GPS trackers for seniors are one of the most anxiety-reducing technologies a Canadian family can invest in, especially when an aging parent lives alone, has been diagnosed with dementia, or is active in ways that sometimes worry you. Unlike medical alert systems (which summon help after something goes wrong), a GPS tracker lets you check on a parent beforeanything happens: and get an alert the moment they leave a safe zone.

Canada's GPS tracker market has its own mix of options: US services like Life Alert don't operate here, coverage varies dramatically between the Rogers, Bell, and Telus networks, and pricing is in Canadian dollars with different plan structures than what you'll find in US reviews. This guide covers only products and services confirmed available in Canada in 2026.

If you're also considering a medical alert system with GPS built in, see our guide to the best medical alert systems in Canada. For seniors who want an all-in-one wearable, we also cover the best smartwatches for seniors in Canada.

Lifeline On the Go is the closest thing Canadians have to a "set it and forget it" GPS safety solution for aging parents. The device is worn as a pendant and uses GPS to track the wearer's location anywhere there is cellular coverage in Canada. When the wearer presses the button, or when the built-in fall detection triggers automatically, a call connects to Lifeline's 24/7 monitoring centre. A trained agent assesses the situation, talks to the senior, and dispatches emergency services if needed.

This is the critical difference between Lifeline On the Go and a basic GPS tracker: you're not just seeing a dot on a map. You're getting a monitored response. For families who cannot always answer their phone in the moment, or for seniors who live alone, that intermediary monitoring centre can be the difference between a fall going unnoticed for hours and emergency services arriving within minutes.

The Lifeline brand has a long history in Canada and is well recognized by family doctors, home care coordinators, and hospital discharge planners. If your parent is being discharged from hospital and you need a GPS safety solution quickly, Lifeline is often the one healthcare workers will recommend by name.

Why Apple AirTag Is Not a GPS Tracker for Seniors

Apple AirTags are inexpensive, easy to find, and frequently mentioned in conversations about tracking seniors, but they are not a GPS tracker and are not appropriate as a primary safety solution for an aging parent, especially one with dementia.

Here's what AirTags actually do: they broadcast a Bluetooth signal that is detected by nearby Apple devices (iPhones, iPads, Macs). When another Apple device detects your AirTag, it anonymously relays its location back to Apple's Find My network. This means:

  • No real-time tracking.You only see where the AirTag was last detected by another Apple device, not where it is right now. In an emergency, a parent could be somewhere completely different from their last known location.
  • No alerts when they wander. There is no geofencing or departure notification feature.
  • No SOS button. Your parent cannot call for help from an AirTag.
  • Depends on nearby Apple devices. In a park, a rural road, or any area without nearby iPhone users, an AirTag may not update for a very long time.

AirTags have a use case: they're good for tracking luggage, keys, or other items. They are not a substitute for a GPS tracker with cellular coverage and an SOS button when your parent's safety is at stake.

Canada GPS Tracker Buying Guide

Which cellular network does it use?

This is the most important question to ask before purchasing any GPS tracker in Canada. The three major national networks are:

  • Telus: Best rural coverage in BC and Alberta. Strong in most urban centres across Canada.
  • Rogers: Best coverage in Ontario, Quebec, and Atlantic Canada. Used by Lil Tracker and many other GPS devices.
  • Bell: Comparable to Rogers in most of eastern Canada. Worth checking Bell's coverage map if Telus and Rogers are weaker in your parent's specific area.

Always look up your parent's home address and the routes they typically travel on each carrier's coverage map before choosing a device. Rural and remote coverage can be dramatically different between carriers.

Monitored vs. unmonitored

A monitored GPS tracker (like Lifeline or Telus LivingWell) connects to a staffed monitoring centre when an alert triggers. An agent speaks with your parent, assesses the situation, and dispatches help if needed. An unmonitored tracker (like Tracki or Lil Tracker) sends a push notification to your phone, and relies on you to answer, call back, and respond. Both approaches are valid, but monitored is significantly more expensive. Consider: if your parent triggers an alert at 3 AM, will you or another family member reliably wake up and respond? If not, monitored is worth the extra cost.

Fall detection

Fall detection automatically triggers an alert if the device detects a sudden impact and change in orientation consistent with a fall. This matters for seniors who may not be able to press a button after a fall. Of the options reviewed, Lifeline On the Go and Telus LivingWell include fall detection. The Lil Tracker Pro model adds fall detection. The basic Tracki and AngelSense (as a GPS tracker) do not include fall detection, though AngelSense has other alerting features suited to dementia.

Contract vs. month-to-month

Some Canadian providers require a minimum contract (12 to 24 months). Others are month-to-month. For elderly parents whose needs change quickly (due to hospitalization, a move to a care home, or declining health) month-to-month flexibility is valuable. Always ask about cancellation terms before signing up.

Bilingual support

If your parent's first language is French, confirm that the monitoring centre offers service in French before signing up. Quebec families should ask explicitly whether operators are bilingual. Most major Canadian monitoring centres offer both official languages, but verify before purchasing.

Battery life

Most GPS trackers for seniors need to be charged daily or every 1 to 2 days. For seniors with memory issues, daily charging becomes another task that may be forgotten or done incorrectly. Consider who will handle charging, and look for devices with a simple charging dock rather than a fiddly cable. Some families place the charging dock on the nightstand and build charging into the bedtime routine.

Provincial Programs & Financial Help

Dedicated GPS tracker subsidies are not widely available through provincial health plans as of 2026. Unlike some hearing aid programs (see our guide to hearing aids in Canada), GPS tracking devices generally fall outside covered assistive technology categories. However, there are some avenues worth exploring:

  • Ontario: Ontario Health at Home (formerly LHIN) coordinates home care services and can connect families with case managers who may know of local programs or funding options. The Assistive Devices Program (ADP) covers some assistive technologies; ask a healthcare provider whether any GPS or personal emergency response device might qualify for your parent's specific situation.
  • British Columbia: BC's Home and Community Care program coordinates support for seniors. A home care coordinator can advise on any available funding for personal emergency response or monitoring devices in your region.
  • Alberta: Alberta Health Services provides information on continuing care supports. Contact AHS or a local seniors' resource centre to ask about any available assistance with personal safety devices.
  • Quebec: Contact your local CLSC (Centre local de services communautaires) for information on support programs for seniors living at home. Some CISSS/CIUSSS regional authorities offer programs that may cover safety monitoring equipment.
  • Atlantic provinces and territories: Programs vary significantly. Contact your provincial Department of Health, a local seniors' centre, or your family doctor for the most current local information.

Important tip: Even if no formal program exists, some private long-term care insurance policies cover personal emergency response systems and GPS monitoring devices as assistive technology. Check your parent's insurance policy or ask their insurance broker before paying fully out of pocket.

The Alzheimer Society of Canada is also a valuable resource for families working through GPS tracking for a parent with dementia. Many provincial chapters have staff who can advise on available local support. Visit alzheimer.ca to find your provincial chapter.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best GPS tracker for seniors with dementia in Canada?
For seniors with dementia in Canada, AngelSense and Lifeline On the Go are the strongest options. AngelSense uses a tamper-resistant clip that prevents removal and is designed specifically for individuals with cognitive impairments. Lifeline On the Go adds 24/7 monitored emergency response with automatic fall detection. Both are available in Canada with monthly plans billed in CAD.
Do GPS trackers for seniors work in rural Canada?
Coverage in rural Canada depends on which cellular network the tracker uses. Devices running on the Telus network generally have the best rural coverage in western Canada (BC and Alberta). Rogers-based devices cover most of Ontario and Quebec well. Always check the coverage map for the specific carrier (Telus, Rogers, or Bell) before purchasing if your parent lives outside a major city.
Are GPS trackers for seniors covered by provincial health plans in Canada?
Dedicated GPS tracker subsidies are not widely available through provincial health plans in Canada as of 2026. Some provinces have home care and assistive technology programs that may partially offset costs in specific circumstances. Contact your provincial Ministry of Health, Ontario Health at Home, or your family doctor for the most current information. Private long-term care insurance may also cover some costs, check your parent's policy.
Can I use an Apple AirTag to track a senior with dementia in Canada?
Apple AirTags are not suitable as a primary tracking solution for seniors with dementia. AirTags do not use GPS. They rely on Bluetooth and nearby Apple devices in the Find My network, which means they only show where the tag was last detected, not where the senior is right now. There is no SOS button and no real-time alerts. A dedicated GPS tracker with cellular coverage and an SOS button is the appropriate choice.
What should I look for when buying a GPS tracker for an aging parent in Canada?
Key considerations: which Canadian cellular network the device uses (determines rural coverage), whether the monthly plan is contract or month-to-month, whether it has fall detection, whether there is an SOS button, battery life, and comfort/wearability. For seniors with dementia, also look for a tamper-resistant design so the tracker stays on. Check whether monitoring is professional (a staffed call centre) or self-monitored (app notification to family).
How much does a GPS tracker for seniors cost per month in Canada?
Monthly costs range widely. A basic data plan for a portable tracker (like Tracki or Lil Tracker) can start around $14 to 20 CAD/month. Medical-alert-grade GPS devices with 24/7 monitoring (like Lifeline On the Go) typically cost approximately $36 to 45 CAD/month. Specialized dementia trackers like AngelSense run approximately $60 to 80 CAD/month. Device purchase costs are separate and range from about $40 CAD for a basic tracker up to $150 to 230 CAD for feature-rich devices. Always verify current rates directly with each provider.

Choosing the Right GPS Tracker for Your Canadian Parent

The right GPS tracker depends on your parent's situation and your family's needs. For most families, the Lifeline On the Go gives the best balance of real-time GPS, fall detection, and professional monitoring at a mid-range monthly cost, and it's backed by a name that Canadian healthcare professionals recognize and trust.

If your parent has been diagnosed with dementia and wandering is a serious concern, AngelSense is worth the higher monthly cost for its tamper-resistant design and dementia-specific features. If you need maximum rural coverage in BC or Alberta, Telus LivingWell Mobile is worth prioritizing. For families on a tight budget who simply want to see where Dad is on a map, the Tracki Mini on Amazon.ca is a practical, low-cost starting point. And if you want a Canadian company with Canadian support, Lil Tracker delivers a solid GPS watch on the Rogers network with no long-term contract required.

Whatever you choose, confirm that the device has cellular coverage at your parent's home and the places they typically go. This is the one factor that can render any tracker useless, and it varies more across Canada than most device reviews acknowledge.
