My father-in-law loves reading but his eyesight has made paperback novels increasingly frustrating. Switching him to a Kobo e-reader — with the font cranked up to large — genuinely changed his daily routine. Here's what I've learned researching Canadian options for families in the same situation.

Choosing an e-reader for an aging parent in Canada is meaningfully different from the US. The most important factor most people don't know: Kindle e-readers do not support Canadian library borrowing. Every public library in Canada — from Vancouver to Halifax — uses a system called OverDrive (and its companion app, Libby) to lend free ebooks. Kobo e-readers have built-in OverDrive integration. Kindle e-readers in Canada do not.

For seniors on a fixed income, the ability to borrow unlimited ebooks for free with a library card is enormous. It's one of the main reasons Canadian librarians, seniors' centres, and long-term care staff overwhelmingly recommend Kobo over Kindle for Canadian readers. This guide explains the full picture — with CAD prices, where to buy in Canada, and which model suits different levels of vision and tech comfort.

Why Kobo Is the Better Choice for Most Canadian Seniors

Kobo was founded in Toronto in 2009 as a Canadian company (the name is an anagram of "book"). It's now owned by Japanese company Rakuten but remains deeply integrated into the Canadian book ecosystem. Here's why it matters for seniors specifically:

Free library ebooks — the single biggest reason

Every Kobo model sold today has built-in OverDrive integration. OverDrive is the platform used by virtually every Canadian public library to lend ebooks and audiobooks digitally. With a valid Canadian library card, your parent can:

  • Browse thousands of ebook titles for free
  • Borrow and have books download directly to the Kobo — no phone needed
  • Place holds on popular titles and get notified when they're available
  • Return books early to borrow others
  • Access audiobooks through the Libby app (on phone or tablet)

Major Canadian library systems with OverDrive ebook lending include Toronto Public Library, Vancouver Public Library, Calgary Public Library, Edmonton Public Library, Ottawa Public Library, Halifax Public Libraries, Winnipeg Public Library, and most regional and provincial library systems. Rural libraries across Saskatchewan, Manitoba, New Brunswick, and Nova Scotia are also part of the OverDrive network. If your parent has a library card — or can get one for free at their local branch — they have access to thousands of books at no ongoing cost.

Kobo integrates with Indigo/Chapters

Indigo is Canada's largest book retailer, and Kobo is their e-reader partner. This means Kobo e-readers and ebooks are prominently sold and supported at Indigo stores nationwide. If your parent needs in-person help setting up their device, an Indigo location is often an accessible resource — something that isn't available for Kindle users.

Accessibility features Canadian seniors value

  • 24 font sizes — including very large settings for macular degeneration or reduced vision
  • Bold text option — makes standard fonts heavier for easier reading
  • OpenDyslexic font — built into all Kobo devices; many seniors with reading difficulties find it significantly easier to process
  • Adjustable line spacing and margins — reduces visual crowding for those with processing challenges
  • Warm amber light — reduces eye strain for evening reading

Setting Up Free Canadian Library Ebooks on a Kobo

One of the most valuable things you can do for an aging parent is set up library ebook borrowing on their Kobo before handing it over. Here's the process:

  1. Get or confirm a library card. Any Canadian library card works. Most libraries issue cards online now, so you may not even need to visit in person. Cards are free.
  2. On the Kobo, go to OverDrive. From the home screen, tap the menu and look for "Get Books" or "OverDrive." Sign in with your library card number and PIN (or create an OverDrive account linked to the card).
  3. Search and borrow. Browse by title, author, or genre. Tap Borrow on any available title — it downloads to the Kobo immediately. Popular titles may have a waitlist; tap "Place Hold" and the Kobo will notify you when it becomes available.
  4. Read offline. Once downloaded, books are available even without Wi-Fi — ideal for rural homes, cottages, or long-term care facilities.

Most Canadian seniors who make it past the initial setup find this process intuitive. The key is the first-time setup — doing it with your parent the first time removes the friction that might otherwise discourage them from using the feature.

  • Indigo/Chapters — Canada's national bookseller; Kobo's primary retail partner

What to look for when buying for a senior

  • Screen size: 6 inches is compact and portable; 7 inches is easier to read at the same font size. If vision is a concern, consider the 7-inch Libra Colour.
  • Physical vs. touchscreen: Seniors with tremors, Parkinson's, or reduced dexterity benefit significantly from physical page-turn buttons. The Kobo Libra Colour is the only current Kobo model with physical buttons.
  • Weight: Kobo Clara models are around 166g. For seniors with arthritis, even this weight matters over a reading session. Compare with a typical paperback at 250–350g.
  • Font size maximum: All recommended models here allow very large font settings. Test with your parent in the store if possible.
  • Waterproofing: All models listed here are IPX8 rated — useful for reading in the bath or near a pool at a seniors' recreation facility.

What about tablets for reading?

Tablets like the iPad or Amazon Fire HD (reviewed in our Canada tablet guide) can run the Kobo and Kindle apps, so your parent can borrow the same library ebooks on a tablet. However, tablets have backlit screens that cause more eye fatigue during long reading sessions, and batteries last hours rather than weeks. Dedicated e-readers with E Ink screens are noticeably more comfortable for sustained reading — and much lighter. If your parent primarily reads books rather than browsing the web, a dedicated e-reader is the better tool.

Audiobooks for Canadian seniors

Many seniors with deteriorating vision find audiobooks more accessible than reading. The Libby app (the companion to OverDrive) provides free audiobook borrowing from Canadian libraries on phones and tablets. Alternatively, our guide to best tablets for seniors in Canada covers devices well-suited for audiobook listening alongside general reading.

Provincial accessibility programs

Unlike hearing aids or medical alert systems, e-readers are generally not covered by provincial health programs. However, some provinces have assistive technology grants or programs for seniors with visual impairments:

  • CNIB (Canadian National Institute for the Blind) — available nationwide; CNIB may provide resources, training, and in some cases equipment support for Canadians with vision loss. Contact your local CNIB chapter for current programs.
  • Ontario Assistive Devices Program (ADP) — primarily covers medical-grade assistive devices; contact the program directly to ask about eligibility for reading aids.
  • BC's Assistive Technology BC (AT BC) — provides consultation and may be able to direct you to relevant programs.
  • Provincial seniors' centres — many offer digital literacy programs that help seniors learn to use e-readers and library apps, often at no cost.

The CNIB also operates a digital library with accessible ebooks specifically for Canadians with print disabilities — this is a separate resource from provincial library OverDrive access and may provide additional titles in accessible formats.

Gifting an e-reader to a parent

If you're purchasing an e-reader as a gift for a parent, consider setting it up before giving it to them:

  • Create a Kobo account in their name and sign in
  • Connect to OverDrive with their library card
  • Download one or two books they'd enjoy so the device feels immediately useful
  • Set the font size larger than default and enable the warm light
  • Write down the account password and Wi-Fi password on a card to keep with the device

A ready-to-use device is far more likely to become a daily habit than one a senior has to configure themselves. Just as we covered in our guide to cell phones for seniors in Canada, reducing setup friction is the single most important factor in whether a tech device gets used long-term.

Our Recommendation for Canadian Families

For most Canadian seniors, the Kobo Clara BW at ~$169.99 CAD is the right starting point. It's affordable, lightweight, easy to read on, and integrates with free ebook borrowing at every Canadian public library. If your parent struggles with touchscreens or has significant vision difficulties, spend the extra $90 on the Kobo Libra Colour — the physical page-turn buttons alone are worth it for many seniors.

The Kindle Paperwhite is an excellent device, but only makes sense for Canadian seniors who are already invested in the Amazon ecosystem and don't plan to use the library. For most Canadian families, the inability to borrow library ebooks makes Kindle a harder recommendation than the equivalent Kobo.

Whatever device you choose, take the time to set it up before handing it over — sign in, connect to the library, and download a first book. That one step dramatically increases the chances your parent will actually use it and enjoy reading more as a result.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can seniors borrow library ebooks for free with a Kindle in Canada?

No. Amazon Kindle e-readers do not support OverDrive or Libby — the platforms used by virtually every Canadian public library — in Canada. Kobo e-readers have built-in OverDrive integration, letting seniors borrow ebooks and audiobooks directly from their library card for free. This is a major reason Canadian librarians and seniors' centres recommend Kobo over Kindle.

What font size can you set on a Kobo e-reader?

Kobo e-readers offer 24 font size settings, from very small to very large, plus control over font weight (bold), line spacing, and margins. Seniors can make text significantly larger than a typical print book. The Kobo also includes an OpenDyslexic font option, which many people with dyslexia or reading difficulty find easier to parse.

Which e-reader is best for someone with macular degeneration or low vision?

The Kobo Libra Colour's 7-inch screen with very large font settings is a strong choice, and its physical page-turn buttons mean seniors don't need to tap the screen precisely. For severe low vision, a tablet with VoiceOver or TalkBack text-to-speech may be more appropriate. Contact your local CNIB chapter — they offer resources for Canadians with vision loss and may be able to suggest additional tools.

Do Canadian public libraries offer free ebook lending?

Yes. Virtually every Canadian public library system — including Toronto Public Library, Vancouver Public Library, Calgary Public Library, Edmonton Public Library, Ottawa Public Library, and most regional systems — offers free ebook and audiobook lending through OverDrive and the Libby app. A valid library card is all you need. Kobo e-readers connect directly to OverDrive without needing a separate phone or computer.

Can an e-reader be used without internet access?

Yes — once books are downloaded, they can be read offline with no internet connection needed. This makes e-readers ideal for seniors in rural areas, cottages, or long-term care facilities with limited Wi-Fi. Wi-Fi is only needed to download new books or borrow library titles.

Where can I buy a Kobo e-reader in Canada?

Kobo e-readers are available at Kobo.com/ca, Amazon.ca, Best Buy Canada, Indigo/Chapters, and Walmart Canada. Kindle e-readers are available at Amazon.ca and Best Buy Canada. Both brands are stocked at major Canadian electronics retailers across all provinces.
