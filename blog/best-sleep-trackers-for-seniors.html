Older adults often do not realize how poorly they sleep. A sleep tracker can show how often someone wakes at night, which can be useful to share with a doctor.

Poor sleep is one of the most overlooked health issues in seniors. Sleep problems such as insomnia, nighttime waking and breathing disruptions are common in older adults, and many people simply accept them as an unavoidable part of aging. A good sleep tracker changes that. It turns vague complaints ("I don't sleep well") into concrete data a doctor can actually use.

But sleep trackers are not all created equal, and many are simply not designed with seniors in mind. Some require nightly charging, fiddly setup, or apps that are too complex. Others are excellent tools that work silently under the mattress while your parent sleeps: no wearables, no effort, no charging.

We researched 10 sleep tracking devices, focusing on ease of use, accuracy, whether caregivers can access the data, subscription costs, and whether the product is realistic for a senior to use independently. Here is what we found.

The Withings Sleep Analyzer is the top choice for seniors who want meaningful sleep data without having to wear anything. It is a slim, flexible pad that slides under the mattress (below the sheet, beneath the mattress itself) and plugs into a wall outlet. Once it is set up (which a family member can do in about 15 minutes), your parent never has to interact with it again. It tracks automatically every single night.

What makes the Withings stand apart from most sleep trackers in this category is the breathing analysis. The pad uses a pneumatic pressure sensor to detect every breath your parent takes throughout the night. It calculates a Respiratory Disturbance Index, essentially a measure of how often breathing is disrupted, and flags nights that show patterns associated with sleep apnea. This is useful clinical data. Several users on verified review platforms report that the Withings Sleep Analyzer was the reason they first investigated sleep apnea in an aging parent, which led to a CPAP prescription and a significant quality-of-life improvement.

The Health Mate app is well-designed and clear. Sleep is broken into light sleep, deep sleep, and REM stages, displayed in a color-coded timeline for each night. The app shows trends over weeks and months, and a family member can link to a parent's account to view their sleep data remotely: a useful feature for adult children who want to keep an eye on a parent's health without being intrusive.

The core tracking features are free with no subscription required. Withings does offer an optional premium subscription for personalized sleep coaching and health programs, but you do not need it to get the nightly sleep stage data, heart rate graphs, and breathing disturbance reports.

Our verdict: The Withings Sleep Analyzer is our top pick for most seniors and their families. It does everything right: nothing to wear, no nightly charging, meaningful health data, and the ability for a family member to check in remotely. At around $169.95 on Amazon, it is a one-time investment with no required monthly fee. If your parent is a sound sleeper who simply wants basic data, start here.

How to Choose the Right Sleep Tracker for Your Parent

1. Wearable vs. Non-Wearable: The Most Important Decision

This is the first question to ask. Non-wearable sleep trackers, under-mattress pads like the Withings Sleep Analyzer or the OZI Sleep, are far easier for seniors to use consistently, because there is nothing to remember to do. The device is always on. Your parent simply goes to bed and the data collects automatically.

Wearable trackers (rings and wrist bands) require the senior to put on and remove the device every day, and to remember to charge it regularly. For seniors with arthritis, poor dexterity, or memory issues, this is a real barrier. That said, wearable devices also track daytime activity, stress, and heart rate, so they provide a more complete health picture beyond just sleep.

2. Does Your Parent Have Dementia or Memory Issues?

If the answer is yes, choose a non-wearable option with caregiver alerts: the OZI Sleep is purpose-built for this. A senior with cognitive decline cannot be expected to remember to wear a ring or charge a wristband reliably. An under-mattress sensor is the only type that can realistically provide consistent data in this scenario. The bed-exit alert is an important safety feature as well, since nighttime wandering is common in dementia.

3. Do You Want Breathing and Sleep Apnea Clues?

Sleep apnea often goes undiscussed with a doctor: NHLBI says 50 to 75 percent of adults with symptoms have not talked to a physician about it. If this is a concern, choose the Withings Sleep Analyzer. Its pneumatic sensor provides the most detailed breathing analysis of any non-wearable consumer device. It will not diagnose sleep apnea (only a physician-ordered polysomnography can do that), but it can generate data that gives a doctor a compelling reason to order a sleep study. Bring 2 to 4 weeks of Withings reports to the next doctor appointment if breathing disturbances are flagged.

4. Subscription Costs Over Time

Many families do not think about this upfront. A $399 Oura Ring with a $5.99/month membership costs about $610 over the first three years (the first month is free). The Garmin Vivosmart 5 at $149.99 with no subscription costs less than half the price of the ring alone. For seniors on fixed incomes, a no-subscription device is worth prioritizing. Our picks with no required subscription: Withings Sleep Analyzer, Garmin Vivosmart 5, and Samsung Galaxy Ring.

5. Family Visibility: Can You See the Data?

One of the most underrated features of sleep trackers for seniors is the ability for a family member to view the data. The Withings Health Mate app allows account sharing so you can check your parent's sleep trends from your own phone. OZI Sleep was designed with caregiver visibility at its core. If your parent is not going to check the app themselves, which is realistic for many older seniors, family access is not a nice-to-have feature, it is essential.

6. Sleep Trackers Are Not Medical Devices

Every sleep tracker on this list is a consumer wellness product, not a medical device. They are excellent for spotting trends, motivating better sleep habits, and giving a doctor useful context, but they do not replace a clinical sleep evaluation. If your parent regularly reports poor sleep, excessive daytime fatigue, morning headaches, or is a loud snorer, those symptoms warrant a conversation with their physician regardless of what any tracker says.

7. Pairing Sleep Tracking with Home Safety

Sleep tracking and home safety are related but separate needs. A sleep tracker tells you how your parent slept. It does not summon help in an emergency. Families who are concerned about overnight safety should pair a sleep tracker with a proper medical alert system, whether that is a wearable pendant device or a smartwatch with fall detection. See our guide to the best medical alert systems for seniors and our comparison of the best smartwatches for seniors for options that complement a sleep tracking setup.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are sleep trackers accurate for seniors?
Sleep trackers are reasonably accurate for detecting sleep duration and general patterns, but they are not medical devices. Wearable trackers tend to overestimate total sleep time if a senior lies still but remains awake. Under-mattress sensors like the Withings Sleep Analyzer are generally more accurate than wrist-based trackers for distinguishing sleep stages. For concerns about sleep apnea or clinical insomnia, a proper sleep study ordered by a physician remains the top choice.
What is the best non-wearable sleep tracker for seniors?
The Withings Sleep Analyzer is our top pick for a non-wearable sleep tracker. It slides under the mattress, requires no charging, and works completely passively once set up. It tracks sleep stages, heart rate, and breathing disturbances. OZI Sleep is a strong alternative if caregiver bed-exit alerts are a priority.
Can a sleep tracker detect sleep apnea in seniors?
Some sleep trackers can detect breathing irregularities consistent with sleep apnea, but they cannot diagnose it. The Withings Sleep Analyzer uses a pneumatic sensor to detect breathing disturbances and generates a Respiratory Disturbance Index. If the device flags repeated disruptions, bring that data to a doctor, but a formal sleep study is required for an official diagnosis. No over-the-counter sleep tracker is FDA-cleared as a diagnostic device for sleep apnea.
Is there a sleep tracker for seniors with no monthly fee?
Yes. The Garmin Vivosmart 5 ($149.99) has no subscription, all features are free in Garmin Connect. The Withings Sleep Analyzer and Samsung Galaxy Ring also have no required monthly subscription. The Oura Ring 5 is the exception. It requires $5.99/month after the trial period to access sleep data in the app.
How can I share my parent's sleep data with their doctor?
Most sleep tracking apps allow you to export a PDF sleep report. The Withings Health Mate app generates detailed reports you can download and email to a physician. Garmin Connect lets you export data as a CSV file. Oura's app allows PDF export of nightly summaries. Bring a 2 to 4 week sleep summary to your parent's next appointment. It gives the doctor a concrete starting point rather than relying on your parent's memory of how they slept.
Can a sleep tracker alert caregivers if my parent gets out of bed at night?
Yes, some sleep trackers include bed-exit alerts. OZI Sleep sends real-time notifications to a caregiver's phone when the sensor detects the bed is no longer occupied. This is particularly valuable for seniors with dementia or a history of nighttime falls. Wearable trackers like the Garmin Vivosmart 5 and Oura Ring do not offer caregiver alerts.
How much sleep do seniors actually need?
The CDC recommends 7 to 8 hours of sleep a night for adults 65 and older. Sleep changes with age, and seniors may wake more often during the night. Getting enough sleep helps lower the risk of chronic conditions including type 2 diabetes, heart disease, high blood pressure and stroke, according to the CDC, so persistently poor sleep is worth raising with a doctor. A sleep tracker can help identify whether disrupted nights are a pattern worth discussing with a doctor.
What is the difference between a sleep tracker and a medical alert system for the bedroom?
A sleep tracker monitors sleep quality (stages, heart rate, breathing patterns) for health awareness. A medical alert system is designed to summon emergency help when something goes wrong. Some bed-exit sensors blur the line, but they are not a replacement for a monitored medical alert system. For seniors who need both sleep insights and emergency safety coverage, we recommend pairing a sleep tracker with a dedicated medical alert system. See our guide to the best medical alert systems for seniors for details.

Our Recommendation

For most families, the Withings Sleep Analyzer is the single best starting point. It is passive, accurate, and generates medically useful data, particularly for detecting breathing irregularities that could indicate sleep apnea. Your parent sets it up once and then never has to interact with it again. At approximately $169.95 with no required subscription, it is a one-time investment that can meaningfully improve your parent's health outcomes.

If nighttime wandering or bed-exit alerts are a priority, look at the OZI Sleep instead. If your parent is comfortable with wearables and wants to avoid any monthly fees, the Garmin Vivosmart 5 is outstanding value. And if only the most accurate sleep data will do, the Oura Ring 5is the top choice: just budget for the ongoing subscription cost.

Whatever device you choose, the most important step is actually reviewing the data together. A sleep tracker sitting on a nightstand generating numbers no one looks at helps no one. Set aside a few minutes once a week to look at the sleep trends with your parent, and bring a report to their next doctor visit. That habit, not the device itself, is what leads to better sleep and better health.
