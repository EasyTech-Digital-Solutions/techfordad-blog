Hearing Aid Buying Guide for Seniors

What changed in 2022 makes this a very different purchase than it used to be. Here's what you need to know before spending a dollar.

By TechForDad · Updated October 2026

Prices & product details change frequently. Always verify current pricing and availability directly with the retailer or provider before purchasing. See our affiliate disclosure.
Health information: general information, not medical advice; it does not replace your parent's doctor, audiologist or pharmacist. Health facts link to their sources, and AI helps draft our guides (how we review products).

Turning the TV up to full volume is often the first sign of hearing loss. The right hearing aid can bring back everyday conversation, and since 2022 many people in the US no longer need a prescription to get one.

What Changed: OTC Hearing Aids Are Now Legal, No Prescription Needed

In October 2022 the FDA created a new over-the-counter (OTC) hearing aid category, allowing adults 18 and older with perceived mild to moderate hearing loss to buy hearing aids in a store or online without seeing a doctor or hearing professional first. OTC hearing aids in this guide cost roughly $200 to $3,000 per pair, compared with several thousand dollars for many prescription hearing aids.

This guide covers both categories honestly, so you can make the right choice for your parent's specific situation.

OTC Hearing Aids: Who They're Right For

OTC hearing aids work well for adults who:

  • Have mild to moderate hearing loss: difficulty hearing in noisy environments, asking people to repeat themselves, turning up the TV
  • Are comfortable with some self-setup: most OTC devices have a companion smartphone app for adjustment
  • Want to try hearing aids at low cost before committing to a prescription device
  • Have relatively symmetric hearing loss: similar loss in both ears

Best OTC options in 2026 (prices are the makers' listed prices; check each page, because they change):

  • ELEHEAR Beyond Pro (about $599/pair): FDA-registered OTC aids with Bluetooth 5.3 streaming, AI noise reduction and a 45-day trial. Our top OTC pick.
  • Jabra Enhance Select M2 ($1,695/pair): FDA-cleared self-fitting OTC aids with three years of remote audiology care and a 100-day trial, according to Jabra.
  • Sennheiser All-Day Clear ($999.95/pair): works with iPhone and Android, with a 90-day trial.
  • Audien Atom Pro 2 ($289/pair): the lowest price here, with no Bluetooth app control and a 45-day money-back guarantee.
  • MDHearing NEO XS PRO ($597/pair): sits inside the ear canal, with a 60-day trial.

No longer sold or supported as before: Sony's OTC models (CRE-E10, CRE-C10, CRE-C20) have been discontinued, and the manufacturer says existing customers keep warranty and service support. LXE Hearing, the company behind Eargo and Lexie, announced in July 2026 that it is winding down its US business, with customer support confirmed through at least September 15, 2026, so we no longer recommend Eargo or Lexie. Jabra Enhance Plus was discontinued on October 1, 2024, with service and support promised until October 1, 2026.

Prescription Hearing Aids: When They're Worth the Cost

Prescription hearing aids, fitted by an audiologist, remain the right choice for:

  • Moderate to severe hearing loss: the FDA says OTC hearing aids are not for the treatment of severe or profound hearing loss
  • Asymmetric hearing loss: significantly different loss in each ear requires custom programming
  • Seniors who've tried OTC and not gotten adequate benefit
  • Complex listening environments: frequent restaurant conversations, large gatherings, or music listening

If your parent needs prescription aids, the major brands to consider are Phonak, Oticon, ReSound, and Widex. Costco's hearing aid centres sell prescription hearing aids at prices that are often lower than private clinics; compare them with an audiologist's quote.

Does Medicare Cover Hearing Aids?

Original Medicare (Parts A and B) does not cover hearing aids or exams for fitting them (Medicare.gov). Some Medicare Advantage plans offer extra benefits such as hearing; coverage varies by plan. Check your parent's specific plan. Some states have Medicaid hearing aid coverage for low-income seniors.

The Veterans Administration (VA) provides hearing aids free of charge to eligible veterans. If your parent is a veteran with hearing loss, this is worth pursuing before spending any money out-of-pocket.

Key Features to Look For

  • Rechargeable batteries: disposable hearing aid batteries are small and challenging for seniors with limited dexterity. Rechargeable devices simplify daily management significantly.
  • Bluetooth connectivity: streams audio directly from iPhone, Android, or TV. Meaningful for seniors who want to hear the television clearly without cranking the volume.
  • Trial period: reputable OTC brands offer 30 to 45 day returns. Do not buy from a source that doesn't offer a meaningful trial period.
  • App simplicity: if your parent will need to adjust settings themselves, the companion app matters. Test it before purchasing.
  • IP rating (water resistance): an IP68 rating means the device can handle sweat, rain, and minor splashes.

Mistakes Most Families Make (and How to Avoid Them)

  • Buying prescription aids at a retail audiologist without comparing Costco: prices differ between clinics, so get more than one quote.
  • Skipping the trial period: hearing aid adjustment takes 2 to 4 weeks. Commit to wearing them consistently before judging whether they work.
  • Buying aids that are too complicated to manage: a technically superior device that your parent can't operate is less useful than a simpler one they'll actually wear.
  • Treating hearing aids as a one-time purchase: hearing loss typically progresses; aids bought for mild loss may not be adequate in 5 years.
